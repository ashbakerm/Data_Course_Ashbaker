The purpose of this project was to understand what factors best predict a graduate’s starting salary and whether they end up pursuing entrepreneurship. My goal was to uncover patterns that students and advisors can actually use to inform decisions around school, career planning, and skill development.The dataset I used came from Kaggle and includes 5,000 recent graduates. It contains 19 variables, including things like GPA, SAT scores, internships completed, soft skills scores, job offers, and whether the graduate became an entrepreneur.To prepare the data, I cleaned up the column names using the janitor package, removed the student ID column, and converted important categorical variables—like gender, field of study, and job level—into factors for analysis.I started with some exploratory data analysis. A few clear patterns emerged:* Graduates with more internships and stronger soft skills tended to earn higher salaries.* Those in STEM majors consistently earned more than others.* There were also some connections between career satisfaction and entrepreneurial behavior.For the salary model, I used multiple linear regression. I built a baseline model using a few theory-based predictors, compared it to a full model, and then used stepwise AIC to select the best-fitting model.The final model had a root mean square error of about $14,000, which means it predicted salary fairly accurately given the diversity of the dataset.For entrepreneurship, I used a logistic regression approach. I used stepwise AIC to simplify the model, and I tuned the classification threshold to around 0.20. That gave me a good balance between identifying entrepreneurs and non-entrepreneurs—around 60% accuracy in each direction.From these models, a few key takeaways stood out:* For salary, the biggest drivers were:o Internships completedo Soft skillso Networking scoreo And of course, your field of study—with STEM majors clearly at the top.* For entrepreneurship, meaningful predictors included:o High school GPAo And career satisfaction
Students should focus on gaining experience through internships. But also, don’t overlook communication and interpersonal skills—those really do translate into higher pay. If you’re interested in entrepreneurship, strong academics and a clear sense of satisfaction with your career direction seem to matter more than just technical skills alone.For advisors and universities, the data suggests that hands-on experiences and soft skill development should be prioritized—especially for students outside of STEM—so we can help close salary gaps and support entrepreneurial potential.Overall, this project gave me a much deeper appreciation for how data can inform personal decisions and educational strategy.
